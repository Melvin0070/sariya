# Rebar Pour Card (the client's engineer photographs the steel cage with a marker stick and gets bar count, diameter and spacing against the drawing before concrete hides it)

- Source: fresh Claude sub-agent (independent brainstorm, Session B, 2026-10-04)
- Status: SHORTLISTED
- Track: Productivity
- Named user, doing a task they already must do: the client-side or third-party quality engineer who must sign the pour card (pre-concreting checklist) for each slab, beam or column, certifying that the contractor's reinforcement matches the bar bending schedule. They are paid to check the contractor; a fail costs them nothing.
- What the capability plugs into (precise: which workflow, system, form, certificate, or paid process): the pour card and the reinforcement check record that releases a concrete pour and supports the contractor's running bill; the evidence file a developer keeps against the five-year structural defect liability under RERA.
- Payer and budget owner: developers' quality heads, project management consultancies and third-party quality auditors; public works departments for bridges and metro viaducts.
- Why now (dated, verifiable, with URL): Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism issued an implementation guideline in July 2023 allowing image-based digital measurement of rebar (diameter, count, pitch) in place of tape-and-marker inspection, and a product category followed: Toda and Hazama Ando adopted an AI rebar inspection service from April 2024, alongside BAIAS and AIJO HiKing (https://www.toda.co.jp/news/2024/20240408_003345.html , https://www.gembaroid.jp/product/baias.html , https://solution.comjo-aijo.com/aijo-hiking/). These need an iPad Pro with LiDAR or a dedicated stereo camera. India has the same hidden-work problem, a five-year structural defect liability on builders (https://www.amsshardul.com/insight/reras-structural-defects/), and no equivalent tool on ordinary phones.
- What the phone does on-device and why it must be local: the engineer lays a printed marker stick (known length, coded targets) on the cage and takes two or three photos. The phone detects bars, separates top and bottom layers using relative depth, measures spacing and count along the marker, classifies diameter into the standard series (8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 25 mm), and compares with the schedule typed or scanned from the drawing. Output is a signed pour-card page with the annotated photo. Local because slabs under construction have no signal (basements, upper floors), the pour truck is waiting, and the photo is the only record once concrete is placed.
- The physical act in the real world that a judge can watch or check: a table-top mat of real bar offcuts (10 mm and 12 mm) tied at 150 mm centres, with the marker stick. The judge slides one bar 40 mm sideways and swaps one 12 mm bar for a 10 mm; the phone flags both. The judge checks with a tape and a vernier caliper on the table.
- Oh-wow moment: the phone circles the one thinner bar that nobody can pick out by eye from standing height, and the caliper confirms 10 mm.
- Physics check (sensor, scale reference, error band) if any measurement is claimed: known-size reference in the bar plane, 1-3%: spacing of 150 mm is good to about ±3-5 mm, enough for a ±10-15 mm tolerance. Diameter steps are 2 mm at the small end; at 1 m distance a 50 MP frame gives roughly 0.3 mm per pixel, but ribs, rust and shadow blur the edges, so 10 versus 12 mm is distinguishable and 8 versus 10 mm is marginal [U]. No ARCore on this phone, so no free-hand metric depth; lower-layer spacing and cover need the marker placed at that layer or a second marker. Lap length and cover are not claimed.
- Known competitors found (with URLs, and the languages searched): Japan: CONSAIT, BAIAS, AIJO HiKing, IHI's drone and phone method (https://ken-it.world/success/2022/05/automatic-rebar-check.html), all for the Japanese guideline and mostly on LiDAR tablets. Research: AR plus ultrasonic rebar tool (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0926580525003280). Bar-counting apps for bundles (CountThings) do not check installed cages. Searched Japanese and English; Chinese and Korean not searched [U].
- Kill criteria for a first pilot: a developer's quality head says pour cards are a formality nobody will pay to tighten; diameter classification on rusty ribbed bar is wrong more than one time in ten; engineers refuse to carry the marker.
- Unverified items [U]: whether any Indian code or tender accepts image-based rebar records; Indian startups already selling this; diameter accuracy on site lighting.

Why-now check: The Japanese half is correct: MLIT's Engineering Affairs Division issued the implementation guideline (draft) for rebar as-built measurement using digital data in July 2023 (Reiwa 5), allowing camera-image measurement at stage inspection (https://www.mlit.go.jp/tec/content/001619475.pdf). The Indian half has no trigger: RERA section 14(3) five-year defect liability is standing law since 2016-17; no Indian code, tender or RERA rule requiring image-based pre-pour records was found. One blog claims "RERA 2.0" brings mandatory third-party construction audits in 2026 (https://www.beyondwalls.com/blog/rera-2-0-india-2026-homebuyer-protection); not confirmed by any primary source, treat as [U]. So "why now" is "Japan proved the category", not an Indian mandate.
Closest competitors: Tokyu "Sma-Easy" (phone + marker, Japan only); IHI consortium (ordinary camera); Modely, CONSAIT Eye, Raku Camera, SiteRebar, BAIAS (depth hardware). None sells in India.
Space left (0-10): 5 in India (cl-11 said 6; lowered because a marker-plus-phone product already exists in Japan and the method is not novel). 1 in Japan.
India-first market: Buyer is the quality head at a developer, a PMC (project management consultancy) or a third-party quality auditor; for bridges and metro, the authority's engineer. Budget line exists (PMC and third-party QA fees are typically a low single-digit percent of project cost), but no line item for pour-card tooling and no mandate forcing one. Rough count: a few thousand organisations (about 200-300 PMC and third-party QA firms plus the quality departments of the larger developers and EPC contractors) [U, own estimate]. Sale is discretionary; the first pilot question in the file ("pour cards are a formality nobody will pay to tighten") is the real risk.
Live-demo wow: Medium. A judge can slide a bar 40 mm and swap a 12 mm for a 10 mm, then check with tape and caliper, so the result is hand-checkable. But a 40 mm shift is visible to the naked eye, and only the diameter catch surprises; it is a measurement demo that jurors (cloud architects) will find competent rather than startling. Fits 3-5 minutes.
Who holds the phone: The client-side or third-party quality engineer, who is paid to check the contractor and is not hurt by a fail. Good. (If the contractor's own site engineer holds it, a fail delays his pour and the incentive reverses.)
